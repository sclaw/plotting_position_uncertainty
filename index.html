Understanding Uncertainty in Order Statistics

Given a finite sample of data, how much should I trust that sample to represent the underlying population? This is one of the fundamental questions of statistical inference. Many people are familiar with methods to get uncertainty around parameters like mean and variance or uncertainty in a fitted distribution, but what about order statistics such as the 50th percentile?

The answer, it turns out, is clean and has a closed form: the Beta distribution. For any finite sample of size n, the probability that the ith smallest element is a certain percentile (u) of the underlying population is given by

$$ f(p) = \frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i)!} p^{i-1}(1-p)^{n-i}, \qquad 0 < u < 1 $$

This, as it turns out, is a beta pdf with $a=i$ and $b=n-i+1$.

This is one of those results that felt too good to be true when I first read about it (shout out to the Handbook of Hydrology). Surely, I thought, this must be a case where they found the distribution was approximately beta through numerical experiments. There's no way this could be derived.

As it turns out, it's actually a very straightforward equation to derive. Let's start with the fact that for any distribution, percentiles are uniformly distributed on [0-1]. Let's consider the 20th percentile, or 0.2 on the support. When we sample from our population, we can consider each sample a yes-no question: is this sample below the 20th percentile? Conveniently, we know the probability of this yes-no coin flip exactly: 0.2 yes and 0.8 no. For the smallest value in our sample to be the 20th percentile, we would need to have exactly n-1 successes. Similarly, for the second smallest value in our sample to be the 20th percentile, we would need to have exactly n-2 successes and 1 failure. Considering a sample of n elements and accounting for any possible ordering of the yes-no experiments, we can calculate this probability as

$$ \Pr(\text{sample is 20th percentile}) = \frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i)!} 0.2^{i-1}(0.8)^{n-i}$$

where i is a given elements rank in the sample. This matches our original formulation.
